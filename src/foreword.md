# Foreword

In 2026 the Cambridge Philosophical Society and Darwin College Cambridge marked the tenth anniversary of David MacKay’s untimely death from cancer. David’s scientific legacy and public service are world-renowned, as reflected during his life by many honours, including a knighthood and his election as the inaugural Regius Professor of Engineering in Cambridge. The biographical memoir published by the Royal Society, written by Malcolm Longair and Michael Cates, provides ample documentation of those achievements and their significance ([Royal Society memoir](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsbm/article/doi/10.1098/rsbm.2017.0013/89327/Sir-David-John-Cameron-MacKay-FRS-22-April-1967-14)).

This meeting was not the first that has been held in honour of David’s work. In 2016 I joined colleagues of his to convene a large meeting of friends, mentors, and scientific collaborators, hosted in the Cambridge Computer Laboratory and at Darwin College. Those events lasted three days, including two days of public lectures, and a further day sharing recent research by alumni of his Inference Group. Much affected by David’s harrowing reports of cancer treatment, we were unsure whether he would be able to contribute, or perhaps even to attend. It proved a remarkable occasion, not least because of David’s final research presentation, recorded for his children as much as for us, based on mathematical principles he had observed in their toys ([2016 memorial meeting programme](https://mlg.eng.cam.ac.uk/djcms2016/#:~:text=16:30-,Surprise%20talk**,-16:30)). It was so characteristic of David to bring us kindness and play in the same conference program alongside recent advances such as those from Geoff Hinton that would subsequently be recognised in a Nobel Prize. Many of those among the convenors and those who joined us for that event became founders and global leaders in the recent AI boom, and on that day they mixed with eminent civil servants, inventors, publishers, journalists and civil society volunteers, not to mention the brilliant and talented members of David’s own family.

The 10th anniversary meeting for which these tributes have been collected was opened by the Vice Chancellor of the University of Cambridge, and was followed by a memorial dinner hosted by the Master of Darwin College. The theme “Energy and Information” chosen by organisers Claire Barlow, Joan Lasenby, Carl Rasmussen and myself recognised David’s remarkable personal energy, as he engaged in so many information campaigns - education, persuasion and advocacy in far more fields of endeavour than the organisers were able to address in one day’s programme ([2026 one-day meeting page](https://www.cambridgephilosophicalsociety.org/media/lectures#:~:text=David%20MacKay%3A%20Energy%20and%20Information%20%2D%20One%2DDay%20Meeting)).

Of course, the words Energy and Information refer not only to David’s personal qualities, but to the areas of research for which he is recognised, and the two astonishing books that he wrote. Energy continues to be one of the most challenging crises humanity faces, and recent applications of AI could certainly benefit from closer attention to the philosophical challenge that introduced David’s PhD thesis – how to apply Occam’s razor to a neural network. In 2026, this would be an excellent demand to make of today’s Large Language Models, especially in the light of the energy they are designed to consume.

The historic character of the one-day meeting in 2026 reflected the 200 year history of our host organisation, the Cambridge Philosophical Society, founded in 1819 for the purpose of promoting scientific inquiry. David recruited me to join the Council of the Society where he and I, along with this event’s co-organiser Claire Barlow, thought of ourselves as “Young Turks”, campaigning for reform and ambition in advancing the Society’s goals. David had recently proposed (unsuccessfully) that the Society should sponsor a novel internet-based system to encourage scientists to talk in person to each other and to the public. David’s idea for “Talks.cam” predated the launch of Facebook, demonstrating the potential that came to be called Web 2.0. Like so many of David’s campaigning ideas, Talks.cam offered an idealistic alternative vision of the role Universities could play in society, and how information systems might transform universities for the better ([Talks.cam history](https://talks.cam.ac.uk/document/who%20we%20are)).

At the time David was elected to be the youngest professor in Cambridge, he asked for his personal chair to be in “Natural Philosophy”, a phrase reflecting not only the original purpose of the Philosophical Society, but Newton’s unification of maths and physics in the Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica - Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy. The unification that David worked to achieve was not of maths and physics, but a mathematical approach to knowledge itself. The phrase Artificial Intelligence had gone out of fashion when he was finishing his PhD, and those words do not appear in the title of his groundbreaking book on information, inference and learning. Nevertheless, his PhD established the Bayesian foundations of neural networks that formed the basis for many of the presentations that can be seen in recordings of the one-day meeting. His Dasher system for predictive text entry was one of the earliest demonstrations of how a generative language model can be usefully applied. The Dasher model is nowhere near as large as the LLMs of today, but I still use Dasher when teaching my own human-centred AI classes nearly 30 years later. David would have been wholly unsurprised by this.

David’s philosophical passion and mathematical aptitude were deeply impressive, but we should also remember that he was building on the work of his father Donald MacKay, one of the UK’s most eminent cyberneticians, who was a key contributor to the original formulation of the goals of AI, and his mother Valerie MacKay’s work in visual psychophysics and neuroscience. These lineages echo the distinctive tradition of great mathematical philosophers who we are privileged to have hosted in Cambridge. Newton, of course, but also John Maynard Keynes, Bertrand Russell or Frank Ramsey, another brilliant figure who died too young, but whose paper on truth and probability I find very reminiscent of David’s concern to find a Bayesian foundation for knowledge.

These are individuals whose contributions and abilities, like David’s, transcended and reconfigured academic discipline. The greatest problems that humanity faces do transcend discipline. It seems that potential solutions, whether energy, AI or other global challenges, demand insights beyond the boundaries of existing degrees and textbooks. How can we maintain the necessary freedom for new generations of talented mavericks to help us overcome them? Many will remember how David chafed against boundaries. He was frustrated by them, but he also understood that we need boundaries, to make places for ideas to thrive, and also that new ideas establish new boundaries, setting up new kinds of critique. Without walls we have no houses.

Perhaps this seems nostalgic, or sentimental rather than scientific, but in the Māori philosophy of my home country they emphasise the important principle of whakapapa. Whakapapa means acknowledging ancestry, where things come from. It also means accepting the responsibility to be a guardian of what you have inherited. David did this thoughtfully and with dedication. The contributors of these tributes, not least myself, owe the same to him.
